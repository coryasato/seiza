# Decisions: Kirkwood

Why Kirkwood is built the way it is. Decisions that bind every app, and the ones that made Kirkwood a showcase and gave it its home, are in the root [`docs/DECISIONS.md`](../../../docs/DECISIONS.md) ("Repo structure", "Hosting").

One bullet per decision, under its area, oldest first: what was chosen, what chose it, and *[where it came from]* (the research, the storyboard's review, a milestone). A decision that replaces another marks the old one *Superseded* rather than deleting it. The research's reasoning is in [`docs/research/asteroid-belt.md`](../../../docs/research/asteroid-belt.md); the storyboard is [the canvas](https://claude.ai/artifact/Vpx3w9Jb6EAW5RFJbLKKoi).

## Scene

- **2026-11-10** · **The table links to the scene four ways:** a row flies the camera to its asteroid; a dot or a drawn group filters the table to its rows (a filter, not a jump: a group's rows are scattered through the file); columns computed from the orbit change as time runs; and "Sort by orbit size" flies the dots from space into a against e. A selection is a set of rows, not a region: its asteroids stay picked as they drift apart. *[Research]*
- **2026-11-10** · **Kepler orbits for the whole belt, a real integration for the Hildas and Trojans only.** N-body over 1.57 M bodies isn't feasible in a tab, and the gaps took millions of years to form, so they aren't shown forming. Over decades either side of the elements' epoch a Kepler orbit looks the same as an integration; the resonant groups are thousands of particles under the Sun and Jupiter, small enough to integrate, and they're where Jupiter's pull is the story. *[Research]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The asteroids only, not Gaia.** A map of the 25 M stars would be 16 times the points, a table that can't live in memory, and nothing that moves. *[Research]*
- **2026-11-10** · **No tour on load.** The scene fills the window and the table is summoned. A visitor opts in through one-press actions ("Sort by orbit size", "Ride with Jupiter", "Find Ceres"), a row of buttons beside the time control. *[Research; storyboard review]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A drawn group is flown in to.** The shape is freehand and picks everything under it through the scene's depth; time pauses while it's drawn. When it closes, the camera flies in until the group fills the scene, and its biggest bodies get a name and diameter. Chosen from three drawn alternatives over staying at the belt's scale and over drawing from the tilted on-load camera: the zoom gives the moment action and focus. *[Storyboard review, board 6's alternatives]*
- **2026-11-10** · **Three.js stays, for now.** As the spike imports it (0.187.0: `WebGLRenderer`, `Points`, `ShaderMaterial`, a line, a camera), its chunk is 523.1 KiB, 104.3 KiB at brotli 11, loaded after GPUI's first frame; the scene's own module is 2.1 KiB beside it, and the spike's wasm 2,772.3 KiB. So it adds under 4% to what the page downloads, none of it before first paint. The belt is one draw and could be written against WebGL2 directly for a few KiB, but what Three.js is here for (bloom, a glowing Sun, trails, camera moves) hasn't been tried yet: weighed again once M2's look says how much of it is used. *[M0 A4]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The elements live in Rust and the scene reads them where they are.** Six f32s a row (a, e, i, and the three angles) in one buffer in the wasm's memory, 37.6 MB for the 1,567,523 rows; the scene's vertex buffer is a view over it, so the upload to the GPU is the only copy, and the table's pages are views over the same rows. The clock is Rust's too: the scene asks it for the time each frame it draws. *[M0 part A]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The scene draws on demand, and its resolution is its own.** Frame times with both canvases drawing (headless Chromium 153, 1440×900 at DPR 2, Apple M1 through ANGLE's Metal renderer, the table's panel open, 4 s a segment, median of 3 runs; a frame is late past 25 ms):

  | | Frame interval p50 / p95 | Late frames | Main-thread work a frame p50 / p95 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | At rest (time paused: the scene draws nothing) | 16.7 / 16.7 ms | 0% | 0.4 / 0.7 ms |
  | Time running | 16.7 / 33.3 ms | 32% | 0.8 / 7.0 ms |
  | The table flinging, time paused | 16.7 / 16.7 ms | 0% | 3.9 / 4.6 ms |
  | The table flinging, time running | 16.7 / 33.3 ms | 37% | 4.7 / 7.1 ms |

  With time running the scene presents about 45 frames a second while the main thread is idle (Three.js's `render()` takes 0.28 ms): the GPU is the limit, not either canvas's CPU, and not the table, which flings at 60 with the scene behind it. One run each of the scene's knobs says what the GPU's time goes to: the scene at half its resolution (one pixel a CSS pixel, the dots the same size on screen) has no late frames, and neither do three quarters of the rows at full resolution; dots half the size, 2 solver steps in place of 6, and an opaque GPUI canvas change nothing. So the cost is points times the scene's pixels, not the orbit math, the fill, or the transparent layer above. The scene's canvas therefore takes a resolution of its own, apart from GPUI's (whose text stays at the display's), and draws only when the clock, the camera, or the window changed. Which resolution ships is chosen by eye in a real browser. *[M0 A3]*

- **2026-11-10** · **The sort flight is the shader's, and one number of Rust's.** Rust holds the flight's progress (0 in space, 1 landed, 3 s end to end) and the scene asks for it each frame, as it asks the clock; the vertex shader places each dot between its place in space and its place in a against e (the storyboard's axes: a 1.5 to 5.5 au across, e 0 to 0.6 up), the inner belt leaving first, so [the columns build from the left](spike/sort-mid.jpg) and [the gaps open as they land](spike/sort-landed.jpg) (the 3:1 gap at 2.50 au reads 14 of 255 against 203 and 236 a tenth of an au each side). No buffer is rewritten and nothing is uploaded: all 1,567,523 points fly on the one draw. Frame times during the flight (the setup of the table above, median of 3): 24% of frames late out and 17% back at the display's resolution, the GPU's limit as with time running, main-thread work 0.8 ms a frame (p95 3.3); none late either way at half the scene's resolution. Landed, the scene draws nothing: time moves no dot there. The clock runs on, so it's no longer moved by the scene's frames: whoever reads it moves it. *[M0 B1]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A drawn group is picked in Rust through the scene's camera, and time stays paused on it.** At the release Rust asks the scene for its camera (projection × view), places every row at the clock's time with the shader's math in f64, and keeps the rows that land inside the shape (its pixels filled once, even-odd, so a row's test is one lookup). For a shape around 150,500 rows that takes 0.36 to 0.42 s on one thread (Chromium, Firefox, and WebKit alike), a stall at the release that M5 has to take off the frame (the rows' rotation computed once, a worker, or the pick spread over the frames the outline closes in). The group is a set of rows: a byte a row to the scene, which grays every other dot, and the rows' indices to the table. Its 95% radius, 1.49 au when drawn, is 2.30 au a year later and 3.57 after three: at a year a second a group drawn in space shears apart before the camera reaches it, so the release leaves time paused (the time control says so, and runs it again; "The whole belt" restores what it was). A shape is drawn in space only for now: in a against e the press does nothing. *[M0 B2]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A closed shape's outline is the scene's.** GPUI draws the shape while it's drawn (window pixels, as the pointer gives them). Closed around a group, its points go to the scene, which puts each where its ray from the camera meets the ecliptic and draws the loop there, so [the outline stays around its dots](spike/fly-in.jpg) as the camera flies in, as the storyboard's board 7 draws it. *[M0 B2]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The scene centers in what the panel leaves.** The scene asks Rust for the panel's width on screen each frame and shifts its picture left by half of it (the camera's view offset), so the belt, a flown-to group, and a against e's axes all sit in the visible part, and move with the panel as it slides. The fly-in's distance fits the group's 95% sphere to the narrower of the window's height and that width. *[M0 B2]*

## Look

- **2026-11-10** · **Color is orbit size.** Every dot is tinted by its own a: rose at the inner edge (1.6 au), pale gold (2.7), blue (3.2), violet at the Hildas (3.97), green at the Trojans (5.2), with dense dust washing toward white. In space it reads as rings, warm inside and blue outside; landed in a against e the colors stand in order, and the Hildas and Trojans tell apart without extra brightness. Chosen over the first drawing's uncolored dust (brightness alone) as more pleasant to look at. The ramp's exact colors are the storyboard's stand-ins (its color note, in oklch) until the scene is built. Weighed: color by class (about 88% of the rows are one class, so the belt stays one color) and by albedo or diameter (expected empty for most rows). *[Storyboard review]*
- **2026-11-10** · **White is for what the interface adds, and gray for what isn't picked.** A drawn shape's outline, a marker, and its path are white, since any accent hue clashes with the ramp somewhere along it (cyan against the outer belt, amber against the inner). Around a picked group the rest of the belt loses its color, so color also means picked. *[Storyboard review]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The belt is glowing dust, not rocks, and the page says its dots are exaggerated.** At true scale an asteroid is an invisible speck millions of kilometers from the next. *[Research]*
- **2026-11-10** · **Up close, a dot grows and brightens with the camera's nearness.** Dots of a fixed size and opacity are dust only where they overlap: flown in to a group they thinned to nothing, and a first correction (opacity by the nearness squared) burned the group white. The dust thins by the square of how much nearer the camera is, so a dot's area and its opacity each grow with the nearness (the size capped at 3 times, the opacity at 12), which keeps the picture's brightness and the ramp's colors. A stand-in like the rest of the look, until M2. *[M0 B2]*

- **2026-11-10** · **A look was tried on the spike, toward astrophotography, and most of it is worth keeping.** Four reference images (a galaxy seen at an angle, a cluster in its haze, two nebulae) set the direction; the rule that held it was that any glow comes from the data. Shot on the real rows from three cameras, as it was and with the look: the opening shot ([before](spike/look-opening-before.jpg), [after](spike/look-opening-after.jpg)), top-down ([before](spike/look-top-before.jpg), [after](spike/look-top-after.jpg)), and flown in to a drawn group ([before](spike/look-group-before.jpg), [after](spike/look-group-after.jpg)); and [landed in a against e](spike/look-sort-landed.jpg), where the gaps still read. Every part is a knob (`spike/src/look.ts`), so the scene as it was is still there to compare. The spike's input and move checks pass with the look on. The bullets below are its parts; M2 builds the look from them. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The scene is drawn in linear light into a half-float buffer and tone mapped once, with Khronos's neutral curve.** That is what lets a dot be brighter than white: dense dust and big bodies go white-hot at the core and keep their color at the edge, with no rule written for it. AgX, the curve first asked for, [grays the ramp](spike/look-opening-after-agx.jpg): it's built to desaturate, and color here is data. Neutral keeps the ramp's hues and only lets go of them near white. The buffer alone costs 2.2 ms a frame at the display's resolution and 0.8 at half. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A dot's brightness and size come from its row's H, as the eye would see it.** H alone doesn't work: it is brightness at 1 au, so the sample's far, large bodies (a past 30 au, H under 8) outshone the whole belt as a field of green lights. Sunlight thins by the square of the distance from the Sun and again on the way to the eye, so each dot's flux is its H's, over those two squares (the Sun's against the main belt's 2.7 au; the eye's against the camera's distance from what it looks at, so a zoom changes nothing). Brightness is that flux to the power 0.45 and size to the power 0.2, a dot of H 17.7 (the median) being the plain one: Ceres is 14 times the plain size, a body of H 13 (15,000 rows are brighter) 2.4 times, and the rest are pinpricks. Nothing is dimmer than 0.4 of plain, so every row is drawn, the 502 with no H among them. A grown dot keeps a core of the plain size inside a wide, faint halo. 0.1 to 0.3 ms a frame. H is one more f32 a row, held in Rust beside the elements (6.3 MB, 2.5 gzipped); in the app it belongs in M1's elements file. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The haze is the dots' own light: the buffer halved three times and blurred, added back.** Taken before the Sun, Jupiter, and the lines are drawn, so nothing but rows (and stars) glows. Weighed: drawing the rows a second time into a small buffer, as first described, which would cost a second pass over 1.57 M points when the GPU is already the limit; the buffer holds the same density, weighted by brightness. 1.1 ms a frame. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **Bloom is kept for what is brighter than white, and it is the look's one real cost.** Three.js's own pass (`UnrealBloomPass`, 2.4 KiB at brotli 11), with its threshold at white, so it spreads the Sun, the biggest bodies, and the brightest stars and leaves the dust alone. [Without it](spike/look-opening-after-nobloom.jpg) the belt is nearly the same and the Sun and stars lose their glow. 5.3 ms a frame at the display's resolution, 1.7 at half; running the bloom at half its own resolution saves 1.2 of the 5.3, so its cost is the full-size passes at either end, not the blur. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The sky is Gaia's: the sample's stars brighter than G 10.5, each in its real direction.** 760,754 stars from Tycho's Gaia file, at infinity (the camera's turn moves them, its place doesn't), brightness and size from G as the dots' are from H, a tint from bp_rp. 6.2 MB (8 bytes a star, 5.6 gzipped), fetched after the belt is up and never waited on; 0.2 to 0.7 ms a frame. Three things it found. The Milky Way does not appear as a band at this cut: the stars crowd toward it and the haze lifts there, no more; a band needs a deeper cut (3.1 M stars under G 12, about 25 MB). The stars have to stay behind the rows: their tints were softened so an orange star isn't read as an inner-belt dot, and they dim to a third while a group is picked, where gray dots and white stars would otherwise be confused. And they sit under the page's text, which wants a scrim or a quieter corner. The stars are JS's, not Rust's: they are not rows of this table. This is a backdrop, not the star map the research turned down. Whether the sky ships is the owner's call: it is the part that leans hardest on "every dot is a row" and "color is data". *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The floor is the page's own background as a tint, and a grain that never moves.** Added after tone mapping, in the display's values. The grain is fixed to the pixel, not animated: the scene draws only when something moved, and a moving grain would have it draw every frame. No measurable cost. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The Sun is a glow, and the opening shot looks across the belt.** The Sun: a small core far brighter than white inside a warm glow that falls off as the square of the distance, plainly not a dot and plainly exaggerated. The camera on load: 24° above the ecliptic (38° before), turned 34° about its line of sight so the belt runs corner to corner, facing ecliptic longitude 290°, toward the galactic center's side of the sky. The pick, the outline, and the fly-in work through any camera: they read the scene's own matrix. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **What the look costs, and what that means for the scene's resolution.** Frames drawn back to back with the clock moving, each waited on until the GPU finished it (so the number is the whole frame, not the display's interval), from the opening camera, median of 3 loads of 120 frames (headless Chromium, 1440×900 at DPR 2, Apple M1):

  | | At the display's resolution | At half |
  |---|---|---|
  | As it was | 24.1 ms | 15.8 ms |
  | The buffer and tone mapping alone | 26.3 | 16.5 |
  | + brightness by H | +0.1 | +0.3 |
  | + bloom | +5.3 | +1.7 |
  | + haze | +1.2 | +1.0 |
  | + floor | −0.2 | +0.2 |
  | + stars | +0.2 | +0.7 |
  | + the Sun's glow | −0.5 | +0.1 |
  | All of it | 34.6 | 19.9 |
  | All but bloom | 30.0 | 18.7 |

  Each part's row is that part alone over the buffer. With time running and the panel open, at half resolution, 12% of frames are late with the look (with or without bloom) where none were before; at rest the scene draws nothing, as before. So the look takes the margin half resolution had, on this machine: M2 chooses the resolution with the look on, by eye and in a real browser, and the first things to give are the bloom's size and the stars' count. First paint is untouched (GPUI's first frame at 173 ms as it was and 170 with the look, median of 9 cold local loads: the same build, and everything the look adds loads after it); the belt is up 29 ms later (395 ms against 366). Bytes: the scene's module 6.4 KiB at brotli 11 (2.1 before) and the add-on 2.4; Three.js's chunk is unchanged at 104.3. Data: 12.5 MB more (H and the stars). *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The color mappings live in one module.** `spike/src/colors.ts` holds each mapping as a table (a value, through stops, to an oklch color): a dot's by orbit size, unchanged, and a star's by bp_rp. The shaders take any table as uniforms, so a new scheme is a new table. The ramp itself is still the storyboard's stand-in and still to be decided. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The full look is kept, the whole Gaia sky with it, at the display's resolution.** The owner looked at it running in a real browser from every camera the knobs give (the opening shot, level, top-down, edge-on, toward the galactic center and away from it, close in) and with a group picked. The full sky (G under 10.5) over the sparse one (G under 8, the second choice) and over none: it is what gives the scene the depth of a solar system. Half the scene's resolution is turned down by eye: it looks bad. So the sky's costs are accepted (dots that aren't rows, a second meaning of color, 6.2 MB, the page's text over stars, which still wants its scrim), and so is the frame rate until M2 works on it: about 29 frames a second while time runs, in headless Chromium on an M1. Three quarters of the resolution is unmeasured. *[Owner, after the look spike]*

## Table

- **2026-11-10** · **The table comes in as a panel on the scene's right, behind a handle on the right edge that carries the row count.** Weighed: the table as a layer under the scene's canvas that toggles front and back, which stays the fallback if GPUI can't draw over the scene. *[Storyboard review; M0 A1 confirms or replaces it]*
- **2026-11-10** · **GPUI's canvas sits over the scene's and shows it wherever GPUI drew nothing; the panel stands.** GPUI on the web configures its canvas opaque (gpui-pre-web 0.3.12, and wgpu's WebGPU backend offers no other mode), but its renderer clears each frame to transparent and blends source-over, so the canvas already holds premultiplied color and a true alpha. Two changes on the host's side make the browser use it, with no fork: the page sets `alphaMode: 'premultiplied'` on the canvas's WebGPU context as GPUI configures it (a WebGL2 context composites that way already), and the app sets the theme token `Root` fills the window with to transparent. The handle, the panel, a gpui-kit menu, and tooltips all draw over the scene ([on load](spike/on-load.jpg), [a menu](spike/menu.jpg), [a tooltip](spike/tooltip.jpg); the control, the canvas as GPUI configures it, is [black](spike/opaque.jpg)). Checked in headless Chromium and WebKit (WebGPU) and Firefox (WebGL2). The layer that toggles front and back isn't needed. It leans on two things GPUI doesn't promise (the transparent clear, the blend), so each gpui-kit bump runs the check again. *[M0 A1]*
- **2026-11-10** · **Live cells are for a person to read.** Which columns are live and how often they refresh waits on the spike, under one constraint: a cell never changes faster than someone can follow. *[Storyboard review; M0 B3]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A filter to a group's rows is the delegate's, and the table needs nothing new for it.** The delegate keeps the group's row indices (a u32 each: 0.6 MB for 150,500 rows) and answers each page through them; the table is told the new count and that every row changed (`set_rows`, then `invalidate_rows`, its `live` feature), and goes to the top. Back to every row is the same two calls. One rough edge: the only way to the top is `jump_to(0)`, which selects the row, so the host clears the selection after. *[M0 B2]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The table arrives with the fly-in.** Both were built (`?arrive=after` is the other). With: the panel slides in over the fly-in's 1.6 s on the same easing, so [the scene moves aside as it zooms](spike/fly-mid.jpg) and the group's rows are readable as the camera arrives, as board 7 draws it. After: the camera arrives, then the panel takes 0.35 s more and the picture shifts a second time. The cost of with is GPUI redrawing the panel every frame of the flight: main-thread work 4.1 ms a frame against 0.8, and at half the scene's resolution 10% of the flight's frames late against 3% (47% against 23% at the display's resolution, where the GPU is already the limit). Each includes the pick's stall. Chosen on the board and the numbers, and by the owner's look at both in a real browser: with is the better of the two. *[M0 B2]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A live column is a redraw of the table, and the distance from the Sun refreshes twice a second.** The column's cells are computed when drawn, from a time that moves on at the column's own rate, and each refresh is one `notify` of the table: no page is read again and no feature is needed. A frame that refreshes costs up to 11 ms of main-thread work, so 2 a second is free (0.8 ms a frame at the median, no frame late at half the scene's resolution) and so is a refresh nearly every frame (3.7 to 5 ms at the median): the rate is a question of reading, not of cost. At a year a second a main-belt row's distance (a 2.7 au, e 0.15) changes by up to about half an au a second, so its second and third decimals are new at every refresh whatever the rate, and only the clock's speed would make them readable while time runs; paused, all three are exact. Twice a second holds each number half a second, long enough to read one row, and is the rate the owner picked by looking. Built to be looked at: a button cycles 1, 2, 4, 10, and as fast as a timer goes (27 to 35 a second), and `?decimals=` sets the digits (3, as the storyboard draws `sun_au`). Distance from the Sun is the one live column until M5; distance from Earth needs Earth's place. *[M0 B3]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The rows are held in Rust, and there is no DuckDB.** The sample's 15 columns beyond a, e, and i went into one file, a column at a time (numbers as 32-bit floats, which write back as the sample's own text for every value of its eight float columns; dates as day counts; the three text columns as their bytes behind 4-byte offsets), and the spike's crate keeps the file's bytes as they arrive and reads each cell where it is ([the table over them](spike/rows.jpg)). Against the same table over DuckDB, which the observatory's GPUI contender is (the same file, table, and machine; `apps/observatory/perf/results/2026-10-18-m4-b-data.json` and `2026-10-19-m4-b-cost.json`):

  | | In memory | Over DuckDB |
  |---|---|---|
  | On the wire | 23.6 MB at brotli 11 (40.2 at gzip 9, 112.8 raw), beside the elements' 8.7 (a, e, and i) | the engine, 5.9 MB, then the 37.7 MB file by range: 1.4 MB a page at the least, 22.4 MB after one fling |
  | In memory | 112.8 MB, the file's own size (the wasm's memory 176.0 MB against 63.2 with the elements alone) | the engine's worker, 59 MB at the open and 111 MB after a fling; 302 MB if the file is loaded into a table (native DuckDB) |
  | The table's first rows, reference | 819 ms from the page's start, the belt up at 447 | 821 ms |
  | The table's first rows, CPU 4× and Fast 4G | 62.9 s, behind 59 MB as the spike serves it (the elements raw, then the rows gzipped; the belt up at 23.1 s); about 36 s for both files at brotli 11, by the link's rate | 13.4 s, with no scene to load beside it |
  | A page of a drawn group's rows | the group's indices, which the pick already made | 5.1 ms from the file, 1.2 from a table (native and on every core: the floor), after the group's 150,000 row numbers are sent in as SQL |
  | A sort of every row | by diameter 305 ms, by `full_name` 700 ms, on one thread, ties in the file's order, for an index of 6.3 MB | a page 54 ms from the file, which reads the column whole over HTTP; 26 ms from a table (native) |

  Median of 3 cold loads each, the rows asked for once the elements are in (`spike/run.ts --only rows`; `spike/sizing.ts` for the bytes and native DuckDB). In memory wins on what the app does, and DuckDB would be a second copy: the scene needs every row's elements in Rust before it draws a dot, a pick answers in row numbers, and the live cells are computed from those elements, so a table over DuckDB would fetch the same rows again in another format, into a worker, behind a bridge that takes only SQL. What in memory costs is the slow link: nothing of the table shows until its whole file is in, where DuckDB shows a page after 7 MB. Accepted for a showcase whose table sits behind a handle and whose belt needs 8.7 MB of elements first either way; M1 decides whether the first rows travel apart from the rest (`PLAN.md`, open questions). So a filter to a group and a sort are both an index the delegate reads its pages through, and `engines/duckdb/` isn't a dependency. *[M0 C1]*

## Stack

- **2026-11-11** · **The stack is the spike's, kept whole.** Each part was tried on it and has its own decision; this is the list.
  - **The scene's renderer is Three.js** (0.187.0), on a canvas of its own under GPUI's, loaded after GPUI's first frame: 104.3 KiB at brotli 11, and 2.4 more for the bloom pass. The question left open at A4 (whether the look would use it) is answered: the look is its half-float targets, its bloom pass, and its tone mapping ("Look"). The belt is one draw of 1.57 M points, each orbit solved in the vertex shader; the scene draws on demand and at a resolution of its own ("Scene").
  - **Rust owns the clock,** and whoever reads it moves it: the scene each frame it draws, the date and the live column at their own rates ("Scene", the sort flight).
  - **Rust owns the selection:** a set of row indices, picked through the scene's camera matrix with the shader's math in 64-bit floats; the scene gets a byte a row, the table the indices ("Scene", a drawn group).
  - **Rust owns the rows:** the elements, six 32-bit floats a row, which the scene's vertex buffer is a view over, and the rest of each row as its file's bytes ("Table", above). The stars of the sky are the scene's own: they aren't rows ("Look").
  - **The two canvases are stacked,** GPUI's over the scene's and transparent wherever it drew nothing, by the canvas's alpha mode set on the page and a transparent background token ("Table"). GPUI's hit test decides which canvas an input is for ("Input"). The table is a panel on the right behind a handle, arriving with a fly-in, and the scene centers in what the panel leaves ("Table", "Scene").
  - **Across the boundary:** the scene asks Rust for plain numbers each frame (the clock, the flight's progress, the panel's width), and Rust calls the scene to move the camera or hand it a group, a view over the wasm's memory that the scene copies before the call returns.

  Not decided here: the scene's resolution and frame rate, the color ramp, and the pick's stall (`PLAN.md`, open questions). *[M0 C2]*

## Data

What the fetch holds, its fields, and its frame: [`data.md`](data.md).

- **2026-11-11** · **Kirkwood's rows are a fetch of its own, every column at full precision, not Tycho's sample joined to the angles.** One fetch of 1,580,272 rows (2026-11-11) holds the sample's 18 columns and the orbit's other elements, so a row's cells and its dot come from one set of numbers. Against the sample (1,567,523 rows, fetched 2026-09-24): every one of its rows is still in the catalog under its `spkid`, and the catalog has 12,749 more. A join would have worked, and would have shown seven-week-old cells beside today's dots: 220 rows have an a, e, or i that moved past what the sample's rounding explains (a on 163, by a median of 0.002 au and at most 160 au; e on 200, at most 0.57; i on 124, at most 3.8°), and 1,662 have more observations in their fit. It would also have left 12,749 known asteroids undrawn, or drawn with no row. The cost: Kirkwood's table isn't byte for byte Tycho's, and has 0.8% more rows. Nothing shared the sample's file anyway: the rows are held in Rust in a file of Kirkwood's own ("Stack"). *[M1 A2]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The scene and the table read one a, e, and i: the elements file's.** The fetch has them at 16 digits; the elements file keeps them at whatever precision it chooses, and the table's cells show those stored values, never a second copy (as the spike's table did). A cell shows no more digits than the stored value holds, so a cell and its dot can't disagree: the constraint the file's precision is chosen under. The sample's own q and period follow from a and e: q = a(1 − e) reproduces JPL's `q` to 1.5 parts in 10^8 at the 99th percentile (worst, 1 in 10^7), and the period from the Gaussian constant and a^1.5 reproduces `per_y` to 2.7 in 10^8 (worst, 3.2 in 10^4, a far object with a kept to few digits); whether the rows file stores them or the table computes them is the rows file's choice. *[M1 A2]*
- **2026-11-11** · **Every row is brought to one epoch at prep: JD 2461200.5 TDB (2026-06-09), the commonest.** 1,483,888 rows (93.9%) are already there. The other 96,384 have 6,376 epochs from 1927 to the fetch's day, half of them more than 6.6 years back; each has its mean anomaly moved on by its own mean motion, M = ma + n × (2461200.5 − epoch), with n the Gaussian constant over a^1.5 (the value the scene computes too; JPL's own `n` agrees to 2.7 parts in 10^8 at the 99th percentile). The other five elements stay as they are. That step is half of them more than 1.5 turns of their orbit, 1% more than 8.8, at most 49. It's a two-body step where JPL would integrate, and it's spent on the rows that can least tell the difference: the off-epoch rows were seen for a median of 6 days and 14 observations (the rest: 6,748 days, 112), and 73,896 of them have their epoch within 60 days of their last observation, so where they are today is unknown by more than the step's error. With one epoch the file holds no epoch column and the scene one clock. Two rows need a rule: (2002 PD153) has no mean anomaly (a circle fitted to two days, its perihelion at its epoch), and takes 0 at its epoch; the 7 with e past 1 have no ellipse and aren't placed (`PLAN.md`, open questions). *[M1 A3]*

## Input

- **2026-11-10** · **GPUI's hit test decides which canvas an input is for.** GPUI's canvas is on top and takes every DOM event; the scene's canvas has no listeners. Under the interface, GPUI draws one window-sized surface that hears what nothing above it took: a drag there is a freehand shape (its points kept in Rust, its outline drawn by GPUI in white, the clock held until the release, which ends it wherever it lands), and a wheel there is handed to the scene through one call into JS. The handle, the panel, and the time control occlude the surface, so a press, a wheel, a hover, or a scrollbar drag on them never reaches it. One hit test means a menu or a tooltip that opens over the scene takes its own input with nothing published to the page. Weighed: routing in the DOM by the interface's published bounds, which every overlay would have to publish too. Checked in headless Chromium, Firefox, and WebKit: a wheel over the scene zooms it and leaves the table, a wheel over the table scrolls it and leaves the scene, a press selects a cell, the scrollbar drags, the table's tooltip opens, and a drag on the scene [draws a shape](spike/shape.jpg). *[M0 A2]*
