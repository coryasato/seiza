# Decisions: Kirkwood

Why Kirkwood is built the way it is. Decisions that bind every app, and the ones that made Kirkwood a showcase and gave it its home, are in the root [`docs/DECISIONS.md`](../../../docs/DECISIONS.md) ("Repo structure", "Hosting").

One bullet per decision, under its area, oldest first: what was chosen, what chose it, and *[where it came from]* (the research, the storyboard's review, a milestone). A decision that replaces another marks the old one *Superseded* rather than deleting it. The research's reasoning is in [`docs/research/asteroid-belt.md`](../../../docs/research/asteroid-belt.md); the storyboard is [the canvas](https://claude.ai/artifact/Vpx3w9Jb6EAW5RFJbLKKoi).

## Scene

- **2026-11-10** · **The table links to the scene four ways:** a row flies the camera to its asteroid; a dot or a drawn group filters the table to its rows (a filter, not a jump: a group's rows are scattered through the file); columns computed from the orbit change as time runs; and "Sort by orbit size" flies the dots from space into a against e. A selection is a set of rows, not a region: its asteroids stay picked as they drift apart. *[Research]*
- **2026-11-10** · **Kepler orbits for the whole belt, a real integration for the Hildas and Trojans only.** N-body over 1.57 M bodies isn't feasible in a tab, and the gaps took millions of years to form, so they aren't shown forming. Over decades either side of the elements' epoch a Kepler orbit looks the same as an integration; the resonant groups are thousands of particles under the Sun and Jupiter, small enough to integrate, and they're where Jupiter's pull is the story. *[Research]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The asteroids only, not Gaia.** A map of the 25 M stars would be 16 times the points, a table that can't live in memory, and nothing that moves. *[Research]*
- **2026-11-10** · **No tour on load.** The scene fills the window and the table is summoned. A visitor opts in through one-press actions ("Sort by orbit size", "Ride with Jupiter", "Find Ceres"), a row of buttons beside the time control. *[Research; storyboard review]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A drawn group is flown in to.** The shape is freehand and picks everything under it through the scene's depth; time pauses while it's drawn. When it closes, the camera flies in until the group fills the scene, and its biggest bodies get a name and diameter. Chosen from three drawn alternatives over staying at the belt's scale and over drawing from the tilted on-load camera: the zoom gives the moment action and focus. *[Storyboard review, board 6's alternatives]*
- **2026-11-10** · **Three.js stays, for now.** As the spike imports it (0.187.0: `WebGLRenderer`, `Points`, `ShaderMaterial`, a line, a camera), its chunk is 523.1 KiB, 104.3 KiB at brotli 11, loaded after GPUI's first frame; the scene's own module is 2.1 KiB beside it, and the spike's wasm 2,772.3 KiB. So it adds under 4% to what the page downloads, none of it before first paint. The belt is one draw and could be written against WebGL2 directly for a few KiB, but what Three.js is here for (bloom, a glowing Sun, trails, camera moves) hasn't been tried yet: weighed again once M2's look says how much of it is used. *[M0 A4]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The elements live in Rust and the scene reads them where they are.** Six f32s a row (a, e, i, and the three angles) in one buffer in the wasm's memory, 37.6 MB for the 1,567,523 rows; the scene's vertex buffer is a view over it, so the upload to the GPU is the only copy, and the table's pages are views over the same rows. The clock is Rust's too: the scene asks it for the time each frame it draws. *[M0 part A]* *Superseded in part, 2026-11-11: the layout is seven columns, not six floats a row ("Data", the elements file); the rest stands.*
- **2026-11-10** · **The scene draws on demand, and its resolution is its own.** Frame times with both canvases drawing (headless Chromium 153, 1440×900 at DPR 2, Apple M1 through ANGLE's Metal renderer, the table's panel open, 4 s a segment, median of 3 runs; a frame is late past 25 ms):

  | | Frame interval p50 / p95 | Late frames | Main-thread work a frame p50 / p95 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | At rest (time paused: the scene draws nothing) | 16.7 / 16.7 ms | 0% | 0.4 / 0.7 ms |
  | Time running | 16.7 / 33.3 ms | 32% | 0.8 / 7.0 ms |
  | The table flinging, time paused | 16.7 / 16.7 ms | 0% | 3.9 / 4.6 ms |
  | The table flinging, time running | 16.7 / 33.3 ms | 37% | 4.7 / 7.1 ms |

  With time running the scene presents about 45 frames a second while the main thread is idle (Three.js's `render()` takes 0.28 ms): the GPU is the limit, not either canvas's CPU, and not the table, which flings at 60 with the scene behind it. One run each of the scene's knobs says what the GPU's time goes to: the scene at half its resolution (one pixel a CSS pixel, the dots the same size on screen) has no late frames, and neither do three quarters of the rows at full resolution; dots half the size, 2 solver steps in place of 6, and an opaque GPUI canvas change nothing. So the cost is points times the scene's pixels, not the orbit math, the fill, or the transparent layer above. The scene's canvas therefore takes a resolution of its own, apart from GPUI's (whose text stays at the display's), and draws only when the clock, the camera, or the window changed. Which resolution ships is chosen by eye in a real browser. *[M0 A3]*

- **2026-11-10** · **The sort flight is the shader's, and one number of Rust's.** Rust holds the flight's progress (0 in space, 1 landed, 3 s end to end) and the scene asks for it each frame, as it asks the clock; the vertex shader places each dot between its place in space and its place in a against e (the storyboard's axes: a 1.5 to 5.5 au across, e 0 to 0.6 up), the inner belt leaving first, so [the columns build from the left](spike/sort-mid.jpg) and [the gaps open as they land](spike/sort-landed.jpg) (the 3:1 gap at 2.50 au reads 14 of 255 against 203 and 236 a tenth of an au each side). No buffer is rewritten and nothing is uploaded: all 1,567,523 points fly on the one draw. Frame times during the flight (the setup of the table above, median of 3): 24% of frames late out and 17% back at the display's resolution, the GPU's limit as with time running, main-thread work 0.8 ms a frame (p95 3.3); none late either way at half the scene's resolution. Landed, the scene draws nothing: time moves no dot there. The clock runs on, so it's no longer moved by the scene's frames: whoever reads it moves it. *[M0 B1]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A drawn group is picked in Rust through the scene's camera, and time stays paused on it.** At the release Rust asks the scene for its camera (projection × view), places every row at the clock's time with the shader's math in f64, and keeps the rows that land inside the shape (its pixels filled once, even-odd, so a row's test is one lookup). For a shape around 150,500 rows that takes 0.36 to 0.42 s on one thread (Chromium, Firefox, and WebKit alike), a stall at the release that M5 has to take off the frame (the rows' rotation computed once, a worker, or the pick spread over the frames the outline closes in). The group is a set of rows: a byte a row to the scene, which grays every other dot, and the rows' indices to the table. Its 95% radius, 1.49 au when drawn, is 2.30 au a year later and 3.57 after three: at a year a second a group drawn in space shears apart before the camera reaches it, so the release leaves time paused (the time control says so, and runs it again; "The whole belt" restores what it was). A shape is drawn in space only for now: in a against e the press does nothing. *[M0 B2]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A closed shape's outline is the scene's.** GPUI draws the shape while it's drawn (window pixels, as the pointer gives them). Closed around a group, its points go to the scene, which puts each where its ray from the camera meets the ecliptic and draws the loop there, so [the outline stays around its dots](spike/fly-in.jpg) as the camera flies in, as the storyboard's board 7 draws it. *[M0 B2]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The scene centers in what the panel leaves.** The scene asks Rust for the panel's width on screen each frame and shifts its picture left by half of it (the camera's view offset), so the belt, a flown-to group, and a against e's axes all sit in the visible part, and move with the panel as it slides. The fly-in's distance fits the group's 95% sphere to the narrower of the window's height and that width. *[M0 B2]*
- **2026-11-11** · **Jupiter is one more two-body orbit from the files' epoch, with its own mean motion.** It isn't a row, so it isn't in the files: its six osculating elements at JD 2461200.5 are JPL Horizons' (body 599 about the Sun, the ecliptic of J2000), held beside the code (`data/jupiter.ts`). Three ways of placing it, against Horizons' own places at eight dates from 50 years before the epoch to 50 after (`data/places.ts`):

  | Jupiter's place from | Worst, within a decade | Worst, within 50 years |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Its elements at the epoch, its own mean motion** | 0.003 au, 0.03° | 0.009 au, 0.08° |
  | Its elements at the epoch, a row's mean motion (the Gaussian constant over a^1.5) | 0.011 au, 0.13° | 0.072 au, 0.83° |
  | JPL's approximate elements with their rates ("valid for 1800 to 2050") | 0.003 au, 0.02° | 0.007 au, 0.07° |

  A row's mean motion counts no mass of its own; Jupiter's is a thousandth of the Sun's, which is 0.05% in its mean motion and most of a degree in 50 years. With its own, the one orbit is as good as the table with rates, with no second kind of clock: the scene places Jupiter with the math it places a row with, and one number more. 0.009 au is under a pixel from the opening camera. *[M1 C1]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The Trojans are the rows of class TJN, the Hildas the rows from 3.7 to 4.2 au, and the files put both where Jupiter says.** Nothing is placed by hand: each row's place is its own six elements', at the epoch (`data/places.ts`; [the Trojans](spike/seen-trojans.jpg), [the Hildas](spike/seen-hildas.jpg), [the belt](spike/seen-belt.jpg), from straight above, the lines toward 60° ahead of Jupiter, 60° behind, and opposite). The Trojans: 16,472 rows, 10,473 ahead of Jupiter with their median 62.6° from it and 5,999 behind at 61.2°; 16,048 of them (97.4%) within 30° of the two points, 17 within 20° of Jupiter itself. The Hildas have no class in the catalog (8,381 of them are OMB, the outer belt's), so they are picked by orbit size: 8,400 rows on an ellipse with a from 3.7 to 4.2 au. The resonance's own angle (three of Jupiter's mean longitudes, less two of the row's, less its perihelion's) says the rule picks the right rows: it would be anywhere for a row the resonance doesn't hold, and 8,045 of the 8,400 (95.8%) have it within 90° of zero. The usual narrower rule (e under 0.3 and i under 20° as well) keeps 7,432 and turns away 807 rows the angle holds, so it isn't used. The triangle's corners (where the rows on the far half of their orbit crowd) point 60.3°, 180.3°, and 300.3° from Jupiter's mean longitude: the three-to-two rule, to a third of a degree. From Jupiter's place they're 5° short of the lines (55.0°, 175.0°, 295.0°), because on that date Jupiter is 5.3° ahead of its mean longitude; the Trojans keep to its place and the Hildas' corners to its mean, so the two only line up twice an orbit. Which Hildas truly librate is a question for the integration that will move them. *[M1 C1, C2]*
- **2026-11-11** · **A row's place is its 2026 ellipse: right at the epoch, close for a decade, and a picture of the belt, not an ephemeris, beyond.** Five known bodies from the elements file against Horizons, which integrates each under the Sun, the planets, and the large asteroids (`data/horizons.json`, `data/places.ts`):

  | | At the epoch | 10 years back | 10 years on | 50 years back | 50 years on |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1 Ceres | 0.0000001 au | 0.044 au | 0.041 au | 0.20 au | 0.17 au |
  | 4 Vesta | 0.0000002 | 0.009 | 0.003 | 0.03 | 0.04 |
  | 153 Hilda | 0.0000009 | 0.12 | 0.09 | 0.40 | 0.10 |
  | 617 Patroclus (a Trojan, behind) | 0.0000008 | 0.02 | 0.07 | 0.65 | 0.76 |
  | 624 Hektor (a Trojan, ahead) | 0.0000002 | 0.13 | 0.02 | 2.7 | 0.18 |

  At the epoch every one is within a millionth of an au (150 km), which checks the frame, the angles' order, the units, and the file's floats in one number. A decade either side, within 0.13 au: about 12 px from the opening camera, up to 2° as the Sun sees it, on a ring 3 au wide. Half a century out a main-belt body is a few degrees along its orbit from where it is, and Hektor, which the two-body orbit doesn't hold near its point, is 30° from its place in 1976. The clouds say the same of every Trojan: 16,048 of them are within 30° of the two points at the epoch, 15,529 ten years on, 12,011 after 50, and 7,630 after a century, with 2,600 beside Jupiter. So the belt as a whole reads the same for decades (the research's claim holds for the picture), no named body is where JPL says far from 2026, and the resonant groups come apart on Kepler orbits within a visitor's reach of the time control: the integration planned for them is needed to keep them, not only to tell their story. How far the time control goes, and what the page says of a far date, is the scene's to decide (`PLAN.md`, open questions). *[M1 C3]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The clock starts today and is held until the asteroids are in.** The date the time control opens on is the visitor's own day (`?date=` and `?paused` are for a screenshot of one date), and it doesn't move while the files arrive: at a year a second, a load on a slow link would otherwise open the belt decades from the day it said. The control says so ("waiting for the asteroids") in place of its speed. Part B moves the release to the belt's first draw; how far the date goes is still B2's. *[M2 part A]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The shader solves Kepler's equation inside a bracket, and is handed the clock in two parts; its arithmetic is run in 32-bit floats against the crate's 64-bit place for every row.** The worry had been the shader's clock (an estimated 0.17 au at worst, "Data"). Run step for step in 32-bit floats (`crate/src/shader.rs`, the 1,580,265 rows with an ellipse, each against `orbit.rs`'s place from the same stored floats; pixels are CSS pixels from the opening camera, counted for the rows in its window), the clock was never the problem and the spike's solver was:

  | Years from the epoch | The spike's shader: one float of years, six Newton steps from M + e sin M | The same clock, the solver bracketed | The scene's: the clock in two parts, the solver bracketed |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 0 | 2.2 au and 30 px at worst, 1 row past a pixel | 0.0004 au, 0.001 px | 0.0004 au, 0.001 px |
  | 10 either way | 4.9 au, 6.9 px, 2 rows | 0.0011 au, 0.06 px | 0.0011 au, 0.06 px |
  | 50 either way (the clock's ends) | 637 au, 31 px, 2 rows | 0.0012 au, 0.011 px; median 0.0000095 au | 0.0012 au, 0.006 px; median 0.0000056 au |

  Six plain steps from M don't converge for a handful of rows with e near 1 close to perihelion (one to three rows in the window past a tenth of a pixel on each date tried, one of them 30 px off at the epoch itself). The scene's solver is the crate's own, in floats: Newton's steps kept inside the bracket bisection would use, from π's side for e past 0.8, until a step moves it by nothing a float holds (32 at most; a typical row takes three or four). With it no row in the window is a tenth of a pixel off anywhere the clock goes, and the worst row anywhere is 0.0012 au, the file's own limit ("Data"). The clock in two parts (whole years and the rest, the row's turns a year split so its product with the whole years is exact) is the "turns taken off before the shader adds the rest": it halves the median and the worst pixel at the clock's ends for four operations, and is kept, though the plain clock would have passed. The test fails if any row in the window is a pixel off at the epoch, a decade either way, or either end. It is an estimate of a GPU (whose sine and cosine aren't this machine's), so one real one is read too: Ceres, Vesta, and Hektor drawn alone are 0.02 to 0.05 px from the crate's place at the epoch and a decade on (`checks/see.ts`, headless Chromium on an M1 through ANGLE's Metal renderer). *[M2 B1, B2]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The clock goes from 1976-06-09 to 2076-06-09, stops at its last day, and starts over.** Fifty years either side of the epoch: the span the files' places were checked against Horizons over (above), where the belt still reads as itself, a main-belt body is a few degrees along its orbit from JPL's place, and a quarter of the Trojans have left their clouds, which is as much as Kepler orbits should be asked to show until the resonant groups are integrated. The shader's arithmetic doesn't set the limit: at the ends its worst row is 0.006 px off. At a year a second a visitor who opens the page today reaches the end in under 50 seconds; the clock stops there, the time control says "as far as 2026's orbits go", and Run starts it over from the day it opened on. A `?date=` past either end opens on the end. Going back before today is `?date=`'s alone: the control runs one way, as board 1 draws it. Weighed: no limit (the clouds halve in a century, and no named body means anything by then); wrapping without stopping (a jump nobody asked for); a line about the orbits' date shown always (the README says it, and the control says it where it matters). *[M2 B2]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The rows with no ellipse are counted apart, not drawn.** 7 rows have e past 1 (interstellar and hyperbolic visitors, 'Oumuamua among them). Drawn on their real paths they'd need a second solver in the shader for seven points that are tens of au outside the picture on any date the clock reaches, placed by a two-body step from a perihelion years gone. So the shader gives them no dot, and the page's line says so in counts taken from the rows the scene was given: "1,580,265 asteroids, each on its own orbit, and every dot is a row. 7 more rows have no dot: they're passing through, on paths that never close." The handle and the table keep all 1,580,272: they are rows. The check counts both from the elements file's own bytes and compares. *[M2 B4]*
- **2026-11-11** · **Jupiter's orbit is the crate's, and the scene asks for its place.** Its elements and its own mean motion sit beside the two-body math in `orbit.rs`, tested against Horizons' eight dates (0.0085 au at worst, and a control with a row's mean motion fails); the scene gets its place each frame the clock moved and its orbit's 256 points once. One implementation, where the spike imported a second one in JS (`data/jupiter.ts`, which the data's own checks still use). *[M2 B3]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The Sun's and Jupiter's names are the page's text, between the two canvases.** A layer of two elements over the scene's canvas and under GPUI's, in the interface's face and muted color, each placed by the scene in the frame that draws its disc ("Sun" centered under it, "Jupiter" beside it, as board 1 has them). Weighed: GPUI text, which would need the scene's camera in Rust every frame and a redraw of the whole interface at the scene's rate (4 ms a frame with the panel open, "Table"), and could trail the disc by a frame; and a texture in the scene, which the look's haze and bloom would then have to be kept off. The names take no input: GPUI's canvas is over them. *[M2 B3]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The scene starts before the asteroids, and time waits for the belt.** The scene's module is asked for with the elements, right after the first frame, and is up first (271 ms against 376 for the elements, local): the Sun, Jupiter, its orbit, and their names are on screen while the files arrive, which on a slow link is most of a minute. The clock's hold moves from "both files held" to the belt's first draw, so Jupiter stands at today's place until the dots join it. If the scene can't start (no WebGL, its module lost), the page says so and the table still opens. *[M2 part B]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The scene ships at the display's resolution, at about 29 frames a second while time runs; nothing short of half resolution buys 60.** Chosen by the owner, by eye in a real browser, over three quarters. The scene's frames a second with time running from the opening camera, the look and the sky on, the panel shut (`checks/see.ts --only rate`: headless Chromium 153, 1440×900 at DPR 2, an Apple M1 through ANGLE's Metal renderer, 4 s a load, the median of 3 loads; a frame is late past 25 ms):

  | The scene at | Frames a second | Late frames | With the bloom at half its size | With no bloom |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | **The display's resolution** | 28.9 | 94% | 29.6 | 35.7 |
  | Three quarters | 40.5 | 48% | 41.4 | 42.7 |
  | Half (turned down by eye, "Look") | 55.9 | 6% | | |

  What each lever is worth at the display's resolution (the median of 2 loads): dots half the size 30.3, no sky 31.1, no haze 31.9, and with no sky, haze, or bloom at all 40.0 (48.9 at three quarters), so the belt's own draw is past a frame by itself and the look is the smaller part. The belt alone at a lower resolution, added over a sky, Sun, and grain kept at the display's, was built and measured: at half it draws 40.5, what the whole scene at three quarters does with no second buffer, and at three quarters 33.7; not kept. Dot size changing nothing while resolution and row count do says the cost isn't the pixels a dot fills. So nothing was given up: the bloom, the dots, and a year a second stay as they were, and the frame rate is accepted, as it was when the look was chosen ("Look"). With the panel open it is 30.4. At rest the scene draws nothing (0 frames in 1.5 s, checked). The owner's next step, not built: a control for the scene's resolution on the page, as Three.js scenes commonly offer, so a visitor can trade sharpness for frames (`PLAN.md`, open questions); `?scale` is the knob it would turn. *[M2 D1]*
- **2026-11-11** · **It runs in Firefox and WebKit, and at DPR 1 the sky reads stronger.** Headless Firefox 155 (GPUI on WebGL2) and WebKit 26.6 (GPUI on WebGPU), 1440×900 at DPR 1, the scene on WebGL2 in both (`checks/see.ts --browser <name> --only cross,wheel`): the first frame, the belt with all 1,580,272 rows as views over the wasm's memory and its counts the file's (1,580,265 and 7), the 779,012 stars, the panel opening on Ceres, a wheel over the scene moving its camera and leaving the table, and a wheel over the table scrolling its rows and leaving the camera, with no page error. First frame at 477 ms in Firefox and 321 in WebKit (one cold load each, unthrottled). Two differences. In Firefox the Sun's name is centered half a pixel off (720.5 where the others have 720): its text is measured half a pixel wider. And at DPR 1, in both, [the stars are much more of the picture](seen/cross-firefox-belt.jpg) than at DPR 2: a star 1.1 px across is one whole pixel at the middle of its profile, where at DPR 2 it's four pixels down its sides. The look was chosen at DPR 2; what it should be at DPR 1 is an open question (`PLAN.md`). *[M2 D3]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The Sun's name on screen is "Sol".** Board 1 writes "Sun"; the owner's change: seen from out here there are many suns, and this one has a name. Only the name beside the disc changes, placed as before (centered, 36 px under it); the prose around the app still says the Sun. *[Owner, M2 part D]*

## Look

- **2026-11-10** · **Color is orbit size.** Every dot is tinted by its own a: rose at the inner edge (1.6 au), pale gold (2.7), blue (3.2), violet at the Hildas (3.97), green at the Trojans (5.2), with dense dust washing toward white. In space it reads as rings, warm inside and blue outside; landed in a against e the colors stand in order, and the Hildas and Trojans tell apart without extra brightness. Chosen over the first drawing's uncolored dust (brightness alone) as more pleasant to look at. The ramp's exact colors are the storyboard's stand-ins (its color note, in oklch) until the scene is built. Weighed: color by class (about 88% of the rows are one class, so the belt stays one color) and by albedo or diameter (expected empty for most rows). *[Storyboard review]*
- **2026-11-10** · **White is for what the interface adds, and gray for what isn't picked.** A drawn shape's outline, a marker, and its path are white, since any accent hue clashes with the ramp somewhere along it (cyan against the outer belt, amber against the inner). Around a picked group the rest of the belt loses its color, so color also means picked. *[Storyboard review]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The belt is glowing dust, not rocks, and the page says its dots are exaggerated.** At true scale an asteroid is an invisible speck millions of kilometers from the next. *[Research]*
- **2026-11-10** · **Up close, a dot grows and brightens with the camera's nearness.** Dots of a fixed size and opacity are dust only where they overlap: flown in to a group they thinned to nothing, and a first correction (opacity by the nearness squared) burned the group white. The dust thins by the square of how much nearer the camera is, so a dot's area and its opacity each grow with the nearness (the size capped at 3 times, the opacity at 12), which keeps the picture's brightness and the ramp's colors. A stand-in like the rest of the look, until M2. *[M0 B2]*

- **2026-11-10** · **A look was tried on the spike, toward astrophotography, and most of it is worth keeping.** Four reference images (a galaxy seen at an angle, a cluster in its haze, two nebulae) set the direction; the rule that held it was that any glow comes from the data. Shot on the real rows from three cameras, as it was and with the look: the opening shot ([before](spike/look-opening-before.jpg), [after](spike/look-opening-after.jpg)), top-down ([before](spike/look-top-before.jpg), [after](spike/look-top-after.jpg)), and flown in to a drawn group ([before](spike/look-group-before.jpg), [after](spike/look-group-after.jpg)); and [landed in a against e](spike/look-sort-landed.jpg), where the gaps still read. Every part is a knob (`spike/src/look.ts`), so the scene as it was is still there to compare. The spike's input and move checks pass with the look on. The bullets below are its parts; M2 builds the look from them. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The scene is drawn in linear light into a half-float buffer and tone mapped once, with Khronos's neutral curve.** That is what lets a dot be brighter than white: dense dust and big bodies go white-hot at the core and keep their color at the edge, with no rule written for it. AgX, the curve first asked for, [grays the ramp](spike/look-opening-after-agx.jpg): it's built to desaturate, and color here is data. Neutral keeps the ramp's hues and only lets go of them near white. The buffer alone costs 2.2 ms a frame at the display's resolution and 0.8 at half. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **A dot's brightness and size come from its row's H, as the eye would see it.** H alone doesn't work: it is brightness at 1 au, so the sample's far, large bodies (a past 30 au, H under 8) outshone the whole belt as a field of green lights. Sunlight thins by the square of the distance from the Sun and again on the way to the eye, so each dot's flux is its H's, over those two squares (the Sun's against the main belt's 2.7 au; the eye's against the camera's distance from what it looks at, so a zoom changes nothing). Brightness is that flux to the power 0.45 and size to the power 0.2, a dot of H 17.7 (the median) being the plain one: Ceres is 14 times the plain size, a body of H 13 (15,000 rows are brighter) 2.4 times, and the rest are pinpricks. Nothing is dimmer than 0.4 of plain, so every row is drawn, the 502 with no H among them. A grown dot keeps a core of the plain size inside a wide, faint halo. 0.1 to 0.3 ms a frame. H is one more f32 a row, held in Rust beside the elements (6.3 MB, 2.5 gzipped); in the app it belongs in M1's elements file. *[Look spike]*
- **2026-11-10** · **The haze is the dots' own light: the buffer halved three times and blurred, added back.** Taken before the Sun, Jupiter, and the lines are drawn, so nothing but rows (and stars) glows. Weighed: drawing the rows a second time into a small buffer, as first described, which would cost a second pass over 1.57 M points when the GPU is already the limit; the buffer holds the same density, weighted by brightness. 1.1 ms a frame. *[Look spike]*

- **2026-11-11** · **Kirkwood's rows are a fetch of its own, every column at full precision, not Tycho's sample joined to the angles.** One fetch of 1,580,272 rows (2026-11-11) holds the sample's 18 columns and the orbit's other elements, so a row's cells and its dot come from one set of numbers. - **2026-11-11** · **The scene and the table read one a, e, and i: the elements file's.** The fetch has them at 16 digits; the elements file keeps them at whatever precision it chooses, and the table's cells show those stored values, never a second copy (as the spike's table did). A cell shows no more digits than the stored value holds, so a cell and its dot can't disagree: the constraint the file's precision is chosen under. The sample's own q and period follow from a and e: q = a(1 − e) reproduces JPL's `q` to 1.5 parts in 10^8 at the 99th percentile (worst, 1 in 10^7), and the period from the Gaussian constant and a^1.5 reproduces `per_y` to 2.7 in 10^8 (worst, 3.2 in 10^4, a far object with a kept to few digits); whether the rows file stores them or the table computes them is the rows file's choice. *[M1 A2]*
- **2026-11-11** · **Every row is brought to one epoch at prep: JD 2461200.5 TDB (2026-06-09), the commonest.** 1,483,888 rows (93.9%) are already there. The other 96,384 have 6,376 epochs from 1927 to the fetch's day, half of them more than 6.6 years back; each has its mean anomaly moved on by its own mean motion, M = ma + n × (2461200.5 − epoch), with n the Gaussian constant over a^1.5 (the value the scene computes too; JPL's own `n` agrees to 2.7 parts in 10^8 at the 99th percentile). The other five elements stay as they are. That step is half of them more than 1.5 turns of their orbit, 1% more than 8.8, at most 49. It's a two-body step where JPL would integrate, and it's spent on the rows that can least tell the difference: the off-epoch rows were seen for a median of 6 days and 14 observations (the rest: 6,748 days, 112), and 73,896 of them have their epoch within 60 days of their last observation, so where they are today is unknown by more than the step's error. With one epoch the file holds no epoch column and the scene one clock. Two rows need a rule: (2002 PD153) has no mean anomaly (a circle fitted to two days, its perihelion at its epoch), and takes 0 at its epoch; the 7 with e past 1 have no ellipse and aren't placed (`PLAN.md`, open questions). *[M1 A3]*

  What each row of the table says. a can't be 16 bits: its error is an error in the period, which grows with the clock, and half the belt is a pixel off within 50 years. e and the mean anomaly can't either: on an orbit with e near 1 a step of a 16-bit mean anomaly is months of travel near perihelion, and the rows it misplaces by au are the far comets-in-all-but-name that were found there. i, the node, and the argument of perihelion can: they only turn the orbit, so their error is never amplified, and 16 bits each stays under a pixel on every row for 7.6 MB less. They stay floats anyway, for three reasons. The cells: the table shows the stored numbers, and a 16-bit i shows two decimals where the float shows seven digits (and, not being a decimal, needn't round as JPL's own does). The bytes don't buy the slow link back: at Fast 4G's megabyte a second the belt is 34 s out against 27, and what would bring it in is drawing rows as they arrive, which no precision changes (`PLAN.md`, open questions). And one type is one reader. The 16-bit rotations are the fallback if those 7.6 MB ever matter more than the digits. The mean anomaly is stored as the turn nearest zero: a float is finest there, at perihelion, where a place leans on it hardest; JPL's 0 to 360 puts one row a pixel off for nothing. The file's error is under what a GPU adds: the same floats through the spike shader's clock, in 32-bit arithmetic (estimated in JS), are 0.000017 au off at the median over the 50 years and 0.17 au at worst, so a finer file would show nowhere. A column at a time, not a row at a time (37.1 MB at brotli 11) and not in byte planes (31.8 MB, the smallest, but no longer readable where it lies): each column is a view for the scene and a cell for the table. H as a float (2.0 MB at brotli 11) over hundredths of a magnitude in 16 bits (1.9): 10,835 rows have a third or fourth decimal. This replaces "six 32-bit floats a row" ("Scene", "Stack"): seven columns, in the file's own bytes. *[M1 B1]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The rows file keeps one number where the spike kept three text columns: 46.3 MB in memory and 11.2 MB at brotli 11, where the spike's was 112.8 and 23.6** (for 0.8% fewer rows). Every `full_name` in the fetch is its row's number, its name, and its provisional designation in parentheses; every `pdes` is the number or that designation; and a designation has one of a few shapes that fit 32 bits in the order they were given out (`data.md`). So the file holds the designation packed (6.3 MB, 2.1 at brotli 11) and the 26,637 names behind the rows that have one (0.45 MB), and prep fails on any row whose text doesn't rebuild exactly. In the spike the three text columns were 54.8 MB (35.9 of text behind 18.8 of offsets) and 8.9 at brotli 11; here they are 6.8 MB and 2.3, with nothing given up but the work of writing a cell, and a sort by designation is a sort of integers, in the order of discovery. Also out: q and the period, computed from the elements file's a and e (within 1.8 and 3.2 parts in 10^4 of JPL's own columns at worst), and H, which rides with the elements. - **2026-11-11** · **Each file travels whole; the table's first rows aren't sent apart.** The first 1024 rows of both files are 37 KB at brotli 11 (24 of elements, 13 of rows), so a page ahead of the rest is nearly free to send. - **2026-11-11** · **The files are pinned by a manifest, as Tycho's samples are, and checked by reading them back.** `data/MANIFEST.json` holds each file's rows, bytes, SHA-256, source, fetch date, and layout; prep is a function of `data/raw/` alone, and a second run wrote the same bytes (`prep.ts --check`). Every run decodes each of the 1,580,272 rows from the built bytes and compares it with the fetch's: text exactly, H, the diameter, and the albedo as the float written at its shortest, the elements as the nearest float. The move to the epoch is checked by a second road, JPL's own mean motion times the days from its perihelion passage: the stored mean anomalies agree to 0.00014 degrees at worst. Prep fails past a hundredth of a degree on 1 row in 1,000, and if q or the period computed from a and e leaves JPL's by 1 part in 1,000. *[M1 B3]*
- **2026-11-11** · **A file is written into the wasm's memory as it arrives, and read there as 32-bit words.** The page knows each file's length from the manifest (which its build imports), asks the crate for room (`file_begin`), and copies each piece of the response's stream to its place; the crate then reads the header and keeps the allocation. So a file is held once: no `ArrayBuffer` of it on the page's side, no parse into a second buffer (the spike's elements were two), and the wasm's memory grows by 90.64 MB for the files' 90.52 (97.26 MB against 6.62 at the first frame; 99.88 with the table open and scrolled end to end; the spike's was 176.0). The room is a `Vec<u32>`: every section starts on a 4-byte boundary, so the allocation is aligned for a view of any column (the scene's attributes, in part B), and a cell is a word's bits, read with no `unsafe` and no copy; a byte or a 16-bit cell is a shift, a name the few bytes of its text. A response that isn't the manifest's length, or a file that isn't one (the magic, the epoch, the sections' sum against the length, the numbered rows' keys, the names' rows and offsets in order, the two files' row counts against each other), is the page's error, said under the title, never a read past a section later. A file's SHA-256 isn't compared with the manifest's: two builds of the same rows would pass for each other. The pieces arriving in order is also what a file cut into blocks of rows would need (`PLAN.md`, open questions). The files are served from `data/` by the app's own Vite plugin, as they are, in dev and preview; where a deploy puts them is M6's. *[M2 A3]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The readers are tested against the fetch, on the real files, through a fixture prep writes.** `data/fixture.json` (committed, 9 KB): for each of the files' rules, the first row of the catalog that shows it (a numbered and named row, a numbered one with no name, an unnumbered one, a designation with `A/`, a survey's, which is the last row, a first observation with only its year, no H, no diameter, a diameter and an albedo, a near-Earth asteroid, the circle with no mean anomaly, a row with no ellipse), with the fetch's own 18 cells and its six elements at the epoch in 64-bit floats. The files are gitignored (a checkout builds them with the fetch and prep), so those tests are `#[ignore]`d: a workspace-wide `cargo test` passes without the files, and `just kirkwood test` runs them (`--include-ignored`). Weighed: a small pair of files of the fixture's rows alone, committed, which would run anywhere but would test a file no visitor is served, and would lose the rule that a row's number is its place. *[M2 A2]*
- **2026-11-11** · **The sky's file is built from Tycho's Gaia sample by a script of its own, pinned beside the other two, and held by the page, not the crate.** `data/stars.ts` reads Tycho's `gaia-dr3-bright.parquet` (checked against the SHA-256 Tycho's manifest pins, which the entry records) and writes `data/stars.bin`: every star brighter than G 10.5, brightest first with ties by Gaia's key, each a direction (three 16-bit integers, a unit vector in the ecliptic of J2000, the frame the elements are in) and two bytes of light (G in twentieths of a magnitude, bp_rp in fiftieths). 779,012 stars, 6.23 MB, 5.38 at brotli 11. ## Input

- **2026-11-10** · **GPUI's hit test decides which canvas an input is for.** GPUI's canvas is on top and takes every DOM event; the scene's canvas has no listeners. Under the interface, GPUI draws one window-sized surface that hears what nothing above it took: a drag there is a freehand shape (its points kept in Rust, its outline drawn by GPUI in white, the clock held until the release, which ends it wherever it lands), and a wheel there is handed to the scene through one call into JS. The handle, the panel, and the time control occlude the surface, so a press, a wheel, a hover, or a scrollbar drag on them never reaches it. One hit test means a menu or a tooltip that opens over the scene takes its own input with nothing published to the page. Weighed: routing in the DOM by the interface's published bounds, which every overlay would have to publish too. Checked in headless Chromium, Firefox, and WebKit: a wheel over the scene zooms it and leaves the table, a wheel over the table scrolls it and leaves the scene, a press selects a cell, the scrollbar drags, the table's tooltip opens, and a drag on the scene [draws a shape](spike/shape.jpg). *[M0 A2]*
